Hi, I’m Felipe Alarcón. I’m an astronomer from Santiago, Chile. I was in fact an engineering student at the Universidad de Chile, back when I first saw the ALMA interferometer’s image of HL Tau my career choice shifted to look upwards. I wanted to know what could make those rings and gaps, and whether a young planet might be hiding in one of them. That image is a big part of why I became an astronomer. I started by studying how gas moves in disks. Then, during my PhD at the University of Michigan, I began asking what the chemistry of that gas could tell us about how disks evolve.
 
I’m now a postdoctoral fellow in Stefano Facchini’s UNVEIL group at the University of Milan. I love it when a model gives me a reason to look somewhere new. My models of how dust obscures young planets led me to search archival observations of disks with faint millimeter emission. In one of those disks, I found EK Cha b, a protoplanet candidate with novel approaches to how planet formation can be understood.

These days, I’m trying to understand how the chemistry of gas changes as it moves through a disk and what that might tell us about the material a planet grows from. I’m also leading a James Webb Space Telescope program that uses light from a background source to look for molecular hydrogen in the disk around V4046 Sgr. I like the idea that something behind a disk can help us work out what’s inside it.

Thanks for stopping by. You can find more about my path in my CV, or explore the papers behind these projects.

Protoplanetary disks are thought to be the birthplaces of planets and as such they are very important in constraining their compositions, their origins, and whether or not they could be Earth-like or host life as we know it.


![MAPS Gallery, Oberg et al 2021, Law et al, 2021](https:falarcon.github.io/assets/images/maps.png "")


Images  from the dust millimetric particles such as the DSHARP Gallery show that these disks are very rich in substructures, which could be interpreted as potential signposts of ongoing planet formation. Recent images from the MAPS Collaboration show line emission from important gas tracers in five of these disks showing they have even richer structures across different molecular species, making them interesting objects to study
