Hi, I’m Felipe Alarcón. I’m an astronomer from Santiago, Chile. I was in fact an engineering student at the Universidad de Chile, back when I first saw the ALMA interferometer’s image of HL Tau my career choice shifted to look upwards. I wanted to know what could make those rings and gaps, and whether a young planet might be hiding in one of them. That image is a big part of why I became an astronomer. Then, during my PhD at the University of Michigan, I began asking what the chemistry of that gas could tell us about how disks evolve.

I’m now a postdoctoral fellow in Stefano Facchini’s UNVEIL group at the University of Milan. I enjoy looking for new approaches to detect protoplanets and push our understanding of planet formation. Thanks to this, I found EK Cha b, a protoplanet candidate with novel approaches to how planet formation can be understood.

Thanks for stopping by. You can find more about my path in my CV, or explore the papers behind these projects.

These days, I’m trying to understand how the chemistry of gas changes as it moves through a disk and what that might tell us about the material a planet grows from. I’m also leading a James Webb Space Telescope program that uses light from a background source to look for molecular hydrogen in the disk around V4046 Sgr. I like the idea that something behind a disk can help us work out what’s inside it.




![DHSARP Gallery, Andrews et al 2018](/assets/images/dsharp.png)


Protoplanetary disks are thought to be the birth places of planets and as such they are very important at constraining their compositions, their origins and whether or not they could be Earth-like or host life as we know it.


![MAPS Gallery, Oberg et al 2021, Law et al, 2021](/assets/images/maps.png)

Images  from the dust millimetric particles such as the DSHARP survey (Andrews et al. 2018) show that these disks are very rich in dust substructures, which could be interpreted as potential signpots of ongoing planet formation. Recent images from the MAPS Collaboration (Öberg et al. 2021) show line emission from important gas tracers in five of these disks, leading to believe that they have even richer and more complex structuress across different molecular species, making them interesting objects to study.
